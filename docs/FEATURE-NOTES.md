# Feature notes

How the app behaves, feature by feature, written for people working on the code. What users read is the documentation at [simplmaker.com/docs](https://simplmaker.com/docs/), whose source is in `website/src/docs/`. When you change how something works, update both.

## Using it

SimplMaker is a normal Mac document app, with New, Open Recent, autosave, versions and one window per part. It opens with a **welcome window** like Xcode's: create a new part, open the demo part, open a file, or pick one of the recent parts listed on the right (double-click or Return). Turn it off with the checkbox at the bottom or in Settings; **Window → Welcome to SimplMaker** (⇧⌘1) brings it back any time. New to CAD? **Make your first part** in the welcome window (or **Help → Tutorial: Make a Desk Organizer**) starts a guided tutorial of about ten minutes: a card at the bottom of the view walks you through making a desk organizer: five named parameters, a sketch and an extrude for the floor, then a second sketch on top of the floor that traces its outline, insets it as a guide, and hangs three sections from the guide with sizes typed as expressions and one Equal constraint, an extrude that raises the walls, and a last step that changes `width` and `depth` and watches the walls and every section follow. What to use next pulses in the toolbar, and the card follows what you actually do: it moves on when the part shows a step is done, steps back if you undo it, and says what is off (a rectangle drawn with plain numbers, a sketch on the wrong face, an inset that went outwards, walls left separate from the floor). The **‹** button goes back a step and undoes it. **Do it for me** does the current step the way the interface would; **Leave tutorial** (or ✕) closes the card, and **Help → Resume Tutorial** brings it back. The steps live in `Sources/SimplMaker/Tutorial/`. **File → Open Example Parts** (just under Open Recent) has three parts to learn from: a plate with a hole, a pin hole cut from a sketch on its top face, two filleted corners and a chamfered edge (⌥⌘N), an L-bracket braced by two ribs, each grown from a sketched line, and an advanced five-body assembly: a manifold block, an O-ring, a flange, a pipe swept along a curved path and four threaded bolts, each body in its own colour.

- **Parameters** (left panel, under the Browser): edit any expression, e.g. set `width` to `90 mm`, or `thickness` to `1 in`, or `hole_d` to `depth / 3`. Press Return and the model rebuilds.
- **Timeline** (the bar along the bottom of the window, toggled from the View menu): select a feature to edit it in the inspector on the right. Right-click a feature to suppress or delete it. A red triangle means the feature failed; hover over it to see why. The features after it still rebuild.
- **Sketch:**
  1. Click **Sketch** in the toolbar (or Sketch → New Sketch…). The three origin planes appear at the origin; click **XY**, **XZ** or **YZ**, or click a flat face of a body. Esc cancels. If a flat face is already selected, the sketch goes straight onto it. The view turns to face the sketch plane. (Sketch → New Sketch On still picks a plane directly.)
  2. Draw with the tools in the toolbar. Keys: **L** line (click point after point; Esc ends the chain), **B** spline (a smooth curve through the points you click; press and drag to pull a handle, click the first point to close the shape), **R** rectangle, **C** circle, **A** arc (center, start, end), **P** point, **D** distance, **I** dimension, **S** select.
     - **F** fillet: click a corner where two lines meet to round it.
     - **T** trim: click the part of a curve to cut away, up to where other curves cross it.
     - **E** extend: click near the end of a line or arc to extend it to the next curve.
     - **Offset:** select curves (lines, arcs, circles, and spline/Bézier runs), then press **Offset**. The offset shows live beside the originals with an arrow on it: drag the arrow to set the distance (drag it back through the curve to go to the other side), or type a number or expression in the field next to it (negative goes the other way). Return or **OK** keeps it as one undo step; Esc or **Cancel** takes it back. A positive distance goes outward on a closed shape, and to the left of an open chain's direction. Lines and arcs stay linked to their originals by a distance dimension; a Bézier's offset is a close fit made of several curves (no dimension), and is refused when the distance is more than the curve's tightest bend allows on the inside.
     - Clicking an existing point connects to it.
     - Clicking a curve puts the new point on that curve.
     - Nearly horizontal or vertical lines snap and get a constraint.
  3. **Dimensions:** with **I**, click a line (then empty space) for its length, a circle for its diameter, an arc for its radius, or two points, a point and a line, or two lines. Type a value or an expression such as `width / 2` in the popover that appears. Click a dimension label to change it later.
     - **Distance:** with **D**, click two items and type the distance between them, for example to put a hole 4 mm from the edge of a rectangle. A point, a line or a circle's center point is measured as usual; a circle or arc is measured from its *edge* (to measure from the middle, click its center point instead). Two points can be measured straight, or only horizontally or vertically, with the buttons in the popover. Two lines must be parallel. The circle moves to keep the distance and keeps its size, and the edge it is measured from stays where it is (likewise the point or line you click second). The distance is drawn with extension lines and arrows; with the Select tool, drag its line to move it out of the way.
  4. **Constraints:** select entities with **S** (⇧-click to add more), then pick from the **Constrain** menu or the inspector: coincident, horizontal, vertical, parallel, perpendicular, tangent, equal, midpoint, concentric, fix. Small symbols next to the geometry show each constraint (**H**, **V**, **∥**, **⊥**, **T**, **=**…). Click one to select it and press ⌫ to delete it. You can hide them under View → Show Constraints.
  5. With the select tool, drag points, lines or circle rims. The geometry follows as far as the constraints allow. ⌫ deletes the selection.
  6. The status bar shows the degrees of freedom left. Curves turn black when the sketch is fully constrained. Press **Return** or **Finish Sketch** when done.
- **Extrude:** select a sketch in the timeline. Its closed regions (profiles) show shaded; click one or more (⇧-click), then press **Extrude**. Drag the orange arrow to set the distance; the model updates as you drag. Or type the distance in the inspector, where you also set the operation (new body, join, cut, intersect) and direction.
  - **Extrude types:** the small arrow beside the Extrude button picks how far it reaches: **One Direction** (a distance to one side of the sketch), **Two Directions** (a distance to each side, with an arrow and a typed field for each; Tab moves between them) or **Mirrored** (one length shared evenly, half to each side). The button then works that way until you pick another, and the inspector's Extent picker changes it afterwards (it also has Through all and To face).
- **Edit a sketch later:** right-click it in the timeline → **Edit Sketch**, or use the inspector. The model is rolled back to that point while you edit.
- **Fillet / Chamfer:**
  1. Click edges in the viewport (⇧-click to add more).
  2. Press **Fillet** or **Chamfer** in the toolbar.
  3. Set the radius or distance in the inspector. It can be an expression like `corner_r / 2`.
  - **Chamfer types:** the small arrow beside the Chamfer button picks how it is measured: **Equal Distance Chamfer**, **Distance and Angle Chamfer** (a distance on one face and the angle to it) or **Two Distance Chamfer** (a distance on each face). The button then works that way until you pick another. The last two show an arrow and a size field for each value (Tab moves between them), and a Flip button (or the inspector's switch) puts the first distance on the edge's other face. With faces picked, the first distance is on the picked face.
- **Loft along a path** (centerline loft): a Loft can take a **Path**, an open chain of sketch curves from the first profile's plane to the last's (`LoftFeature.path`, picked with the Loft tool after the profiles, or in the Inspector; `path_sketch` + `path` on the AI tool `create_loft`). The kernel (`sk_loft_along`) cuts the path at those two planes and sweeps the sections along it with OCCT's pipe shell; every profile stays where it was drawn. Where the path meets every profile within 3° of a right angle the sections stay square to the path and turn with it (constant binormal: the path sketch's normal). Otherwise the profiles must be parallel, and the sections stay parallel to them and only move sideways. The reason for the two modes: OCCT's turning sweep through sections that stand at a slant to the path thins out and then collapses to zero volume while still reporting a valid solid, so a slanted path with non-parallel profiles is an error, and a result with next to no volume for its size is refused. Holes in the profiles follow the same path. "Straight sides" does not apply. This is not a guide-rail loft (rails on the surface); that would need the side surfaces built from a curve network.
- **Other sketches while sketching:** the sketches shown in the browser are drawn behind the one being edited (`DocumentModel.backgroundSketches`, the inactive sketch colour, pick id 0 so clicks go through), and their points and curves, flattened onto the sketch plane, are added to `SketchReferences` without a model edge, so the cursor snaps to them by position only (no projection, no constraint). A curve seen edge-on becomes one segment. Switch: **Show Other Sketches** in the sketch's Inspector (`showOtherSketches`, on by default, stored inverted as `hideOtherSketches`). **Project Edge** (U) also takes curves of other sketches: a `SketchProjection` whose `edge.body` is a sketch's id and `edge.edge` a curve's id (no new file field; a sketch makes no body, so the ids can't clash). `Regenerator.sketchCurveSnap` says how the curve lands on the plane (line; circle/arc on a parallel plane as itself; circle/arc/ellipse edge-on as the straight stretch it covers; Bézier exactly, and a slanted circle as Bézier pieces), and `followProjections` refreshes it on every rebuild as the kind it was made as. Only sketches earlier in the timeline are offered (their curves become clickable while the tool is on). A sketch's `references` do not list what it projects (true for body edges before this too), so reordering the source after the sketch gives a rebuild error rather than being prevented.
- **Twist Sweep** (⇧W): pushes sketch profiles straight along the sketch's normal for a **Length** while turning them about that line, from a **Start angle** to an **End angle**. The **Curve** (default 1) spreads the turn: at the fraction *t* of the way it is start + (end − start) × *t*^curve. **Slide across / up** moves the profile sideways in the sketch's x and y over the length (a lean, or a blade's rise), and the profile turns about its own middle unless a turning point is set. It is `TwistSweepFeature` in CADCore, built by `sk_twist_sweep` in the kernel: a smooth loft (`BRepOffsetAPI_ThruSections`) through a copy of the profile about every 3° of turn (8 to 80 sections), which `sk_loft` and the twist share through `loftFaces`. Join, cut and intersect work as for a sweep, so a twisted plate intersected with an extruded outline is a fan blade. The angle always turns about the sketch's normal, so a flipped sweep keeps its sign. No drag handle yet.
- **Navigation:**
  - Trackpad: two-finger scroll pans, ⇧ + scroll orbits, pinch zooms, rotate spins the view (in a sketch it turns the sketch on its paper).
  - Mouse: drag on empty space orbits, right- or middle-drag pans, the wheel zooms. In a sketch, ⌥-drag turns the sketch on its paper (the pointer takes the picture round with it; add ⇧ to land on every 15°), the two curved arrows by the view cube turn it a quarter at a time, and the cube's faces and the house turn it upright again.
  - **Settings → Navigation** (⌘,) changes all of it: **Navigate like** copies the mouse buttons and keys of Blender, Fusion, Onshape or SolidWorks; **Customize buttons and keys** sets what each mouse button, the trackpad's scroll and the wheel do with each of ⇧, ⌃ and ⌥ held; and there are switches to invert orbit and pan (up/down and left/right separately) and zoom, plus orbit and zoom speed.
  - The **View** menu has Fit All (⌘0), Top/Front/Right/Home views (⌘1–⌘4), Orthographic Projection (⌥⌘O) and Show/Hide Inspector (⌥⌘I). Esc clears the selection.
- **Keys:** the Create, Modify and Inspect menus show a single letter for each tool: **E** Extrude, **R** Revolve, **W** Sweep (⇧**W** Twist Sweep), **L** Loft, **B** Rib, **P** Pipe, **X** Box, **Y** Cylinder, **G** Sphere, **U** Torus, **K** Coil, **N** Construction Plane, **V** Canvas, **F** Fillet, **C** Chamfer, **H** Hole, **D** Thread, **S** Shell, **O** Project, **M** Move (and rotate), **Z** Scale, **J** Combine, **Q** Mirror, **T** Pattern, **I** Measure, and **A** for Appearance. While you sketch, the letters pick sketch tools instead, and in a text field they type as usual.
- **Body colours:** press **A** (or **View → Show Appearance**) to open the Appearance panel. Select bodies in the view (a face or edge counts for its body) or in the panel's body list (⇧-click to add more), then click a colour, or choose any colour with **Custom Colour**. **Default** puts the standard colour back. Right-click a body → **Appearance…** opens the panel for it. Colours are saved in the file, can be undone, and need no rebuild.
- **Light and dark:** the viewport follows light and dark mode, and draws thicker lines when Increase Contrast is on.
- **Files:** documents are `.simplmaker` files (JSON). They store only parameters and features; geometry is always rebuilt. **File → Export…** writes the bodies to STL or STEP, as one part or one file per body.
- **Threads:** **Modify → Thread** (D), or right-click a round face → **Thread…**, then click the side of a shaft or the wall of a hole. It cuts a modeled thread, external on a shaft and internal in a hole, in the size that suits the face: an 8 mm shaft gets M8, a 6.8 mm hole (the M8 tap drill) gets an M8 thread. The inspector picks the standard (ISO Metric, ISO Metric Fine, UNC or UNF) and size, and shows the pitch, diameters and tap drill. The thread runs the full length of the face or a set distance (drag the arrow), from the free end (a shaft's tip, a hole's mouth) or the other end, right- or left-handed. The face is turned or bored to the thread's diameters first, and the thread uses the basic 60° profile, without tolerance classes.
- **Project:** **Modify → Project** (O) throws sketch profiles onto a body like a projector, so they follow its surface, flat or curved: text on a cylinder, a logo on a sphere. Click the profiles (the letters of a text, say), then click the face to project onto, or press Return to use the whole body when it is the only one. The profiles land along the sketch's normal (or against it: **Flip direction** in the inspector, set to point at the body when you start) on the faces that face the sketch, so a round post gets them on its sketch side only. **Emboss** raises them by the height and joins them to the body; **Deboss** cuts them in by the depth. Drag the arrow on the surface or type the height or depth (an expression works). The inspector can limit the projection to picked faces and pick the profiles again. It is a projection, not a wrap: the shapes are not unrolled, so they stretch where the surface turns away from the sketch, and nothing lands on a face seen edge-on.
- **Import STEP and STL:** **File → Import → STEP…** (⇧⌘I) or **STL…** adds a `.step`/`.stp` or `.stl` file to the timeline as an **Import** feature: one body with all the file's solids, named after the file, in millimetres whatever unit the file uses. The file's contents are stored in the part, so it rebuilds without the original. Fillet, cut, shell, move or rotate it like any other body. **Replace File…** in the inspector swaps in a new version (STEP or STL); features after the import keep their edges and faces as long as the new file lists its faces in the same order. An STL mesh must be watertight (closed, no gaps) and is read in millimetres; its triangles are sewn into a solid and the ones lying in one plane are merged back into single faces, so a box is six faces, not twelve triangles. Curved shapes stay faceted, one flat face per triangle, and meshes of more than 60,000 triangles are refused.
- **Canvas (reference image):** **Create → Canvas (Reference Image)…** (**V**), or **Insert Image…** in the right-click menu of a face or construction plane. Select a flat face or plane first (or click one while the tool waits), then choose a PNG, JPEG, HEIC or TIFF file. The picture lies on that plane at its middle, upright as the view shows the plane, to trace over and measure against. The Inspector sets its **opacity** (a slider; it rebuilds nothing), width (the height follows the picture's proportions), position and turn, all as expressions, and can swap in another file. Clicks and sketching pass through it. The file is kept inside the part, and the browser lists it under Canvases, where the eye hides it.
- **Import SVG:** **File → Import → SVG as Sketch…** turns an SVG file into a sketch on the selected flat face or plane (the XY plane when nothing is selected), or adds it to the sketch you are editing. Paths (lines, arcs, cubic and quadratic curves), rectangles, circles, ellipses, polygons and polylines are read, with groups, `transform`s and `<use>` applied, and sized in millimetres from the file's `width`/`height` and `viewBox` (96 px to the inch when it has no size). The drawing's middle goes on the sketch origin. Circles and circular arcs stay exact; closed shapes become profiles you can extrude or cut. Text and images are left out, so convert text to outlines first. The result is plain, unconstrained geometry that you can dimension and edit like anything you drew yourself.
- **Move manipulator:** **Move** (M) puts one manipulator, for both moving and rotating, on the bodies or sketches you picked: three arrows slide along X, Y or Z, the three squares slide in a plane, the dot in the middle slides across the screen, and the three rings turn about X, Y or Z (drag a ring's dot). The part you grabbed last is lit, and its value is in the field beside it: type a distance or an angle there, or switch between Move and Rotate and X, Y, Z in the panel below. Distances and angles snap to round steps while you drag. It is one **Move** feature with X, Y, Z and Rotate X, Y, Z values, and the turns happen about the middle of what moves, X then Y then Z, before the move; a value that comes from a parameter asks before a drag replaces it with a number. Rotate features from older files still open, with their single arrow.
- **Scale:** select bodies (click a face, or pick them in the browser) or a sketch (in the browser, or click one of its profiles or curves), then **Scale** in the Modify group (or **Z**, or right-click → Scale Body / Scale Sketch). It adds a **Scale** feature with a factor you can type, or drag with the arrow. Bodies grow evenly about the middle of their bounding box (several together about the middle of them all) and keep their faces and edges, so later features still hold; handy for an imported STL or STEP file at the wrong size. A sketch scales about its own origin, including circles and text, and features built from the sketch after it use the scaled version. The sketch itself stays as drawn, so its dimensions still work, and the factor can be a parameter (`width / 40`).
- **Move and Scale inside a sketch:** the sketch toolbar's **Transform** group (also in the **Sketch** menu) works on the curves and points you have selected, or on the whole sketch when nothing is selected. **Move** puts a manipulator in the sketch plane on the selection: drag an arrow to slide along the sketch's x or y, the square or the dot to slide in both, or the ring to turn about the middle of the selection. The sketch follows live; let go to keep it (one undo step), or type an X, Y and angle in the fields under the manipulator and press Return. Pick another selection and it moves to it; Esc or the tool's button again turns it off. **Scale** still opens a box for a factor (about the middle of the selection, or the sketch origin). Circle radii, text height and the dimensions that measure what grows go along when scaling, and a quarter turn swaps horizontal and vertical constraints. A constraint or dimension that would pull the geometry back (a fixed point, or a distance from the origin) puts it back with a message. Text moves, scales and turns (its baseline turns about its anchor, and the angle is saved with it). A new or reopened sketch starts with the Select tool. The **Scale** tool in the Modify group instead adds a Scale feature to the timeline.
- **Pattern inside a sketch:** **Pattern** in the sketch toolbar's Transform group copies the selected curves (or the whole sketch, with nothing selected) in a **Rectangular** grid or a **Circular** run. The copies show live and stay **linked**: they are rebuilt from the originals, so moving, resizing or dimensioning an original, or changing a parameter, moves every copy. For a grid, drag the arrow along x or along y to set the spacing between columns or rows, and type the counts and spacings in the fields (the original counts as one; a negative spacing goes the other way). For a circle, drag the ring to set how far the copies spread (360° goes all the way round, less puts the last copy at the end of the angle), type the count, and click a sketch point to copy around it (the sketch origin to begin with). Counts, spacings and the spread can be expressions or parameters (`n`, `width / 2`); dragging replaces the expression with a number. Return or **OK** keeps the pattern as one undo step; Esc or **Cancel** takes it back. To change a pattern later, click one of its copies with the Select tool; **Delete** (or ⌫) in the panel removes the copies and leaves the originals. The copies are read-only: you can't draw to them, constrain them or drag them, but you can extrude their profiles like any other. A turned copy of text turns with it. A pattern makes at most 400 copies, and curves added to the sketch later are not part of it.
